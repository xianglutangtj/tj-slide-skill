# Questions to ask

Good slides depend on things only the user knows. Ask early, ask little, ask in plain words.

## How to ask

- First read what you were given. Do not ask what the request, the source files or an earlier deck already answer.
- Send the remaining questions in **one message**, numbered, most important first. Aim for 3–6.
- Give each question a default the user can accept with one word: "If you don't say, I'll assume …".
- If the user says "just make it", build with the defaults and list the assumptions in your report.
- Use the user's own language and words. No design jargon ("visual hierarchy", "reveal state").

## Before building

### Always needed (ask if unknown)

1. **Who is in the room, and what do they already know?**
   *Decides which words need defining and which background can go.* Default: informed colleagues who have not followed the details.
2. **When you finish, what should they know, decide, or do?** One sentence.
   *This becomes the spine of the deck; anything that does not serve it is cut.* No default — ask.
3. **How long do you have, and is this a whole talk or one part of a longer one?**
   *Sets the number of slides, and whether there is a title slide or a handoff.* Default: 5 minutes, one section, no title slide.
4. **Which material should I work from, and which numbers and wording in it are final?**
   *Stops draft figures being shown as settled.* Default: everything in the latest file is current; anything marked draft is shown as draft.

### Ask when the content calls for it

5. **Is anything unfinished or uncertain — and is it fine to show it as "not done yet"?** (for status and progress decks)
6. **Who does what next? Please give the names exactly as they should appear.** (when there are next steps)
7. **What came up most recently that is new, as opposed to promised earlier and still open?** (when a meeting has just happened)
8. **Is there anything that should not be shown to this audience?** For example, material prepared for another group, or work not yet agreed with a co-author.
9. **Is the audience being asked to decide something today?** If yes, what exactly, and what are the options?
10. **Who speaks before and after you?** (when the deck is one part of a longer session)

### Look and delivery (ask only if it could change the build)

11. **Should it match an existing deck, template or institution's colours?** Default: white background, navy and one warm highlight.
12. **How will you present — full screen from a browser, a shared link, or exported to PDF?** Default: browser, full screen, offline. Note: click-by-click reveals do not survive a PDF export.
13. **Do you want speaker notes, and in which language?** Default: yes, brief, same language as the slides.

### When something in the source is unclear

Ask rather than guess. Typical cases: a person's name spelled two ways, a word in a transcript that makes no sense, two counts that disagree, a step marked done with no evidence.

> In your notes the reviewer appears as both "Sara" and "Sarah". Which should go on the slide?

## After the first draft

Send the rendered contact sheet or the deck, then ask two or three of these — whichever fit:

1. **Which slide still has too many words?**
2. **Is there a slide you would skip when speaking?** (It probably should not exist.)
3. **Does any click show too much at once, or too little to be worth a click?**
4. **Is there a word here your audience would not use?**
5. **Did I put anything on a slide that you would rather just say?**
6. **Is anything shown as settled that is not, or the other way round?**
7. **Are the names, numbers and the split of work right?**

## After the user edits or another tool revises the deck

Read the new version before touching it. Keep its improvements, do not revert them, and ask only about changes whose intent is unclear.
